Chapter Test: 20-Item HOTS Multiple Choice

Higher-Order Thinking Skills test — items require analysis, evaluation, inference, and application, not recall. Click "Answer & Explanation" to reveal the key.

1. A person says, "I want money to buy a house, so my family is secure, so we can be happy together." Aristotle would identify the true "highest good" in this chain as:

  1. The money.
  2. The house.
  3. The flourishing (happiness together) sought for its own sake — eudaimonia.
  4. Security only.
Answer & Explanation

C. Money and the house are means; flourishing is the end desired for itself. Aristotle's chain of "for the sake of" terminates in eudaimonia. A, B, D are intermediate goods.

2. Aristotle says "one swallow does not make a summer." Applied to the good life, this BEST means:

  1. Flourishing is a single peak experience.
  2. Eudaimonia is assessed over a whole life, not a momentary high.
  3. Happiness is impossible.
  4. Birds predict the weather.
Answer & Explanation

B. The line stresses that flourishing spans a complete life. A is the opposite; C overreads; D is literalist.

3. A student parties every night for pleasure but neglects health, study, and friendships. Aristotle's critique is that this life:

  1. Perfectly achieves eudaimonia.
  2. Mistakes a by-product (pleasure) for the goal and neglects virtuous activity over a whole life.
  3. Is Stoic.
  4. Shows practical wisdom.
Answer & Explanation

B. Pleasure accompanies good activity but is not the goal; the life lacks virtue and long-term flourishing. A, C, D misapply the concepts.

4. "We become brave by doing brave acts." The deepest implication for the good life is that:

  1. Virtue is inborn and fixed.
  2. Character is built by habit and practice, so a good life is cultivated, not merely inherited.
  3. Only naturally gifted people can be good.
  4. Knowing about courage is enough.
Answer & Explanation

B. Aristotle's habituation doctrine makes virtue trainable by anyone. A and C contradict it; D confuses knowing with doing.

5. A soldier who charges into hopeless danger for no reason exhibits which vice, per the doctrine of the mean?

  1. Cowardice (deficiency).
  2. Recklessness (excess) — courage's vice of excess.
  3. Temperance.
  4. Generosity.
Answer & Explanation

B. Too much fearlessness at the wrong time is recklessness, the excess opposite of cowardice. C and D are unrelated virtues.

6. Why is the golden mean "relative to us" rather than a fixed midpoint?

  1. Because virtue is arbitrary.
  2. Because the right amount depends on the person and situation, discerned by practical wisdom, not by arithmetic.
  3. Because there is no such thing as excess.
  4. Because everyone should act identically.
Answer & Explanation

B. Phronesis fits the mean to circumstances. A and C misread Aristotle; D denies the situational point.

7. A student uses the golden mean to evaluate social media. The virtuous mean is BEST described as:

  1. Total abstention from all technology (deficiency).
  2. Compulsive, boundaryless use (excess).
  3. Practically-wise use — the right amount, for the right ends, preserving focus and relationships.
  4. Whatever maximizes engagement.
Answer & Explanation

C. The mean lies between Luddite refusal and compulsion. A is deficiency, B and D are excess.

8. Epicurus is often misread as endorsing crude indulgence. In fact he taught that the good life is:

  1. Endless luxury and excess.
  2. Simple, sustainable pleasures, friendship, and freedom from anxiety (ataraxia).
  3. Pure self-denial.
  4. Wealth accumulation.
Answer & Explanation

B. Epicurean hedonism prized modest, lasting pleasures and tranquility. A is the misreading; C is closer to asceticism; D is not his view.

9. A person stays calm about a canceled flight, reasoning "I control my response, not the airline." This BEST exemplifies:

  1. Hedonism.
  2. Stoicism — focusing on what is "up to us" and accepting what is not.
  3. The function argument.
  4. Scientism.
Answer & Explanation

B. The dichotomy of control is the core Stoic practice. The others name unrelated positions.

10. Feynman argues that understanding a flower scientifically:

  1. Destroys its beauty by reducing it to chemistry.
  2. Adds layers of wonder (cells, evolution, physics of color) to its beauty.
  3. Proves beauty is an illusion.
  4. Is irrelevant to the good life.
Answer & Explanation

B. Feynman's point is that knowledge enriches wonder rather than draining it. A and C are the scientism-adjacent misreadings he rejects; D contradicts his view of science as a good.

11. "Science gives power but does not carry instructions on how to use it." The BEST inference is that:

  1. Science should decide all ethical questions.
  2. Questions of value and the good require ethics and philosophy, which science cannot replace.
  3. Science is worthless.
  4. Power and ethics are the same.
Answer & Explanation

B. Feynman separates the descriptive (science) from the normative (ethics). A is scientism; C and D distort his balance.

12. A commentator claims, "Only science gives real knowledge; ethics and art are just opinion." This is an example of:

  1. Practical wisdom.
  2. Scientism — treating science as the sole source of truth and value.
  3. Aristotelian virtue.
  4. The golden mean.
Answer & Explanation

B. Elevating science to the only valid knowledge is the definition of scientism. The others are unrelated.

13. The Filipino concept of kabutihang panlahat most directly challenges which idea of the good life?

  1. That flourishing is relational.
  2. That the good life is a purely private, individual, consumer achievement.
  3. That community matters.
  4. That virtue is a habit.
Answer & Explanation

B. The public-good idea rejects a purely individualistic good life. A and C are what it affirms; D is compatible with it.

14. A new app maximizes personal convenience but weakens face-to-face community ties. A kapwa-centered ethic evaluates it by asking:

  1. Only whether it benefits the individual user.
  2. Whether it serves the common good and the flourishing of one's community, not just the self.
  3. Only whether it is profitable.
  4. Only whether it is technologically advanced.
Answer & Explanation

B. Relational ethics widens the question to the community. A, C, D each stop at a partial, self- or market-centered criterion.

15. The "attention economy" is analyzed in this chapter as, in Aristotelian terms, primarily selling us:

  1. Virtuous activity.
  2. The vice of excess — shallow, endless pleasures in place of flourishing activity.
  3. Practical wisdom.
  4. External goods.
Answer & Explanation

B. Infinite engagement is the excess vice, opposed to the mean. A and C are its opposites; D miscategorizes it.

16. Which statement BEST integrates Heidegger (Ch. 4) with this chapter's view of the attention economy?

  1. The attention economy has nothing to do with Heidegger.
  2. It is "enframing" at the level of the mind — revealing human attention (and the person) as a resource to be extracted — and the Aristotelian answer is the practically-wise mean.
  3. It exemplifies bringing-forth.
  4. It is a form of the saving power.
Answer & Explanation

B. Attention-as-resource is standing-reserve applied to mind; the mean is the constructive response. A denies the link; C and D invert the concepts.

17. At the personal level, the sugar case illustrates the good-life tension MOST clearly as:

  1. Virtue vs. knowledge.
  2. Immediate pleasure vs. long-term flourishing/health, calling for the mean (temperance) informed by science.
  3. Stoicism vs. hedonism only.
  4. Wealth vs. poverty.
Answer & Explanation

B. Sugar pits pleasure against health (an external good), resolved by temperance guided by knowledge. The others misframe the tension.

18. Why does the chapter say the sugar case reaches the "public good" and "sustainability," not just personal choice?

  1. Because sugar is tax-free.
  2. Because its production and consumption implicate health-system costs, labor conditions, marketing to children, and environmental impact — a shared, not merely private, matter.
  3. Because sugar is only an individual issue.
  4. Because sustainability is unrelated to food.
Answer & Explanation

B. The case ties personal pleasure to community and planet. A is irrelevant; C and D deny the connections the chapter draws.

19. A food company markets sugary drinks aggressively to children. The BEST good-life critique combines:

  1. Only that sugar tastes good.
  2. Aristotle's mean (temperance is hard to habituate when excess is engineered) and the public good (harm to community health, exploitation of the vulnerable).
  3. Only that the company is profitable.
  4. That science forbids all sugar.
Answer & Explanation

B. It joins personal-virtue and common-good analysis. A and C are shallow; D misstates science's role (it informs, not dictates).

20. Synthesizing the chapter, which is the MOST defensible thesis about the good life and technology?

  1. Technology is the good life, so maximize it.
  2. The good life is flourishing — a shared, virtuous, reasoned activity — which science and technology can serve as means but can never define or replace; living well requires examined, practically-wise choices.
  3. The good life is impossible in a technological age.
  4. Pleasure and the good life are identical.
Answer & Explanation

B. It integrates eudaimonia, the mean, Feynman's limits, and kapwa. A is scientism/technophilia; C is defeatist; D is the hedonist error the chapter rejects.

Reinforcement Exercise (Assignment): 20-Item HOTS Multiple Choice

Take-home reinforcement. Same HOTS orientation, new angles on the chapter. Keys with explanations are collapsible for self-checking or teacher-only printing.

1. Aristotle allows that flourishing needs some "external goods" (health, friends, resources). The BEST reading of this is that such goods are:

  1. The whole of the good life.
  2. Necessary conditions for flourishing, but not flourishing itself; beyond a sufficient level they add little.
  3. Irrelevant to a good life.
  4. More important than virtue.
Answer & Explanation

B. Externals enable flourishing without constituting it — anticipating the hedonic-treadmill finding. A, C, D misstate their role.

2. Why does Aristotle call eudaimonia "self-sufficient and complete"?

  1. Because it needs nothing and no one.
  2. Because it is the final end, desired always for itself and never merely as a means to something further.
  3. Because only the rich can attain it.
  4. Because it is a momentary feeling.
Answer & Explanation

B. Completeness means it is the terminus of all our ends. A overstates (Aristotle values friends/community); C and D contradict his account.

3. A person is technically skilled at making money but cannot judge how to live well. Aristotle would say they may have cleverness but lack:

  1. Wealth.
  2. Phronesis (practical wisdom) — knowing how to live rightly.
  3. External goods.
  4. Pleasure.
Answer & Explanation

B. Practical wisdom differs from technical cleverness. The others are not the missing capacity.

4. Which pairing correctly matches a vision of the good life to its characteristic danger?

  1. Stoicism → restless craving for stimulation.
  2. Hedonism → the treadmill of stimulation and craving.
  3. Aristotelianism → enslavement to what we cannot control.
  4. Meaning-based → mistaking pleasure for the end.
Answer & Explanation

B. Hedonism's danger is restless craving. The others are scrambled (that danger belongs to hedonism, control-enslavement to non-Stoics, etc.).

5. Modern psychology's distinction between "hedonic" and "eudaimonic" wellbeing most closely tracks the difference between:

  1. Pleasure-based vs. meaning/flourishing-based accounts of the good life.
  2. Rich vs. poor.
  3. Science vs. religion.
  4. Ancient vs. modern.
Answer & Explanation

A. Hedonic = pleasure; eudaimonic = meaning/flourishing — echoing Epicurus vs. Aristotle. The others are unrelated dichotomies.

6. Research finding: after basic needs are met, more money adds little to wellbeing. This BEST supports which chapter claim?

  1. Money is the highest good.
  2. External goods are conditions, not the substance, of flourishing (the hedonic treadmill).
  3. Poverty is good for the soul.
  4. Virtue is inborn.
Answer & Explanation

B. The finding matches Aristotle's "external goods" and Chapter 4's treadmill. A contradicts it; C and D are unrelated.

7. Feynman's stance is BEST described as a balance that avoids two errors. Those errors are:

  1. Believing science is beautiful vs. believing it is useful.
  2. Seeing science as value-destroying/cold vs. seeing science as the arbiter of all value (scientism).
  3. Doing experiments vs. reading books.
  4. Loving flowers vs. studying them.
Answer & Explanation

B. He rejects both the "science kills wonder" claim and scientism. The other pairs are not the errors he addresses.

8. A Stoic and an Aristotelian both prize virtue, but they differ MOST on:

  1. Whether virtue matters at all.
  2. How much external goods (health, friends, fortune) contribute to a good life — Aristotle counts them as needed conditions; Stoics treat them as "indifferents."
  3. Whether pleasure is the highest good.
  4. Whether reason exists.
Answer & Explanation

B. The classic contrast: Aristotle needs some externals; Stoics say virtue alone suffices. A and D are shared; C is the hedonist question.

9. The chapter ties Chapter 3 (nation-building) to the good life through the idea that:

  1. Individual and common good are unrelated.
  2. A good society is a condition for good individual lives, and vice versa (kabutihang panlahat).
  3. Only nations, not persons, can flourish.
  4. Technology alone builds the good life.
Answer & Explanation

B. The relational view binds personal and public flourishing. A denies the link; C and D overstate.

10. A "digital minimalist" deliberately keeps a phone for genuine tasks but removes attention-harvesting apps. In this chapter's terms, they are seeking:

  1. The vice of deficiency.
  2. The vice of excess.
  3. The practically-wise mean between deprivation and compulsion.
  4. Scientism.
Answer & Explanation

C. Curated, purposeful use is the mean. A would be total refusal; B is compulsion; D is unrelated.

11. "The unexamined life is not worth living" is used in the chapter to argue that the good life:

  1. Happens automatically.
  2. Must be consciously thought about and chosen, not merely drifted into.
  3. Requires a philosophy degree.
  4. Is only for ancient Greeks.
Answer & Explanation

B. Socrates' line grounds the chapter's call to examine and choose. A is the opposite; C and D are false.

12. Which is the STRONGEST reason a "free relationship" with technology (Ch. 4) and the "golden mean" (Ch. 5) point to the same practical stance?

  1. Both demand total rejection of technology.
  2. Both reject the extremes (refusal and domination/compulsion) in favor of aware, purposeful, balanced use.
  3. Both are forms of scientism.
  4. Both ignore ethics.
Answer & Explanation

B. Gelassenheit and the mean converge on balanced, aware use. A misstates both; C and D are false.

13. A company says its feed is "just giving people what they want." Evaluated by the chapter, the BEST response is:

  1. Agree fully; desire-satisfaction equals the good life.
  2. Note that satisfying engineered cravings (excess) is not the same as serving flourishing; the good life judges desires, not just satisfies them.
  3. Say technology is neutral.
  4. Say pleasure is always bad.
Answer & Explanation

B. The chapter distinguishes satisfying desire from flourishing. A is the hedonist error; C dodges; D overcorrects.

14. Aristotle would classify "pleasure" in the good life as:

  1. The goal and definition of flourishing.
  2. A natural accompaniment of virtuous activity and a genuine part of a good life — but not its organizing aim.
  3. Always to be avoided.
  4. Identical to virtue.
Answer & Explanation

B. Aristotle gives pleasure a real but subordinate place. A is hedonism; C is asceticism; D conflates distinct things.

15. The sugar case is called "a microcosm of the good-life question" chiefly because it:

  1. Involves only nutrition.
  2. Stages pleasure-vs-flourishing simultaneously at the personal, social/public-good, and planetary levels.
  3. Is unique to the Philippines.
  4. Has no ethical dimension.
Answer & Explanation

B. Its three-level structure mirrors the whole chapter. A, C, D understate or deny its scope.

16. The history of sugar labor in the Philippines (haciendas, sacadas) is raised to show that consumption choices:

  1. Have no connection to justice.
  2. Are embedded in a web of labor and social justice — the public-good dimension of the good life.
  3. Are only about taste.
  4. Concern only the wealthy.
Answer & Explanation

B. It surfaces the justice/common-good layer. A, C, D deny or shrink it.

17. Which best explains why Feynman's humility about science's limits is itself a form of wisdom?

  1. Because it rejects science.
  2. Because recognizing that science cannot settle questions of value keeps ethics in charge of ends and guards against scientism.
  3. Because scientists should not do experiments.
  4. Because value questions are meaningless.
Answer & Explanation

B. Knowing the boundary of one's method is intellectual and practical wisdom. A misreads him; C and D are false.

18. A society with abundant technology but weak community bonds would be judged by the chapter as:

  1. Fully flourishing, since technology suffices.
  2. Deficient in the good life, because flourishing is relational (kapwa/kabutihang panlahat) and cannot rest on technology alone.
  3. Irrelevant to ethics.
  4. Ideal by Aristotle's standard.
Answer & Explanation

B. Relational flourishing requires community, not just tools. A and D ignore the relational and virtue conditions; C is false.

19. The chapter's overall relationship between science/technology and the good life is BEST captured as:

  1. Science/technology is the good life.
  2. Science/technology are powerful means that the good life (the end) must judge and direct.
  3. Science/technology are enemies of the good life.
  4. They are unrelated.
Answer & Explanation

B. Means judged by ends — the chapter's throughline from Ch. 4. A is technophilia/scientism; C is technophobia; D is false.

20. Applying the whole chapter, the wisest response to "we can now do almost anything with technology" is:

  1. "So we should do everything we can."
  2. "'Can' does not settle 'should'; we need an examined vision of the good life — virtue, the mean, the common good — to judge which powers to use and how."
  3. "Technology will decide for us."
  4. "Nothing we do matters."
Answer & Explanation

B. The is/ought and means/ends distinctions, plus virtue and the common good, guide judgment. A is technophilia; C abdicates agency; D is nihilism.

Essay: The Future of Chapter 5's Topics (3 Items)

Each item includes a short model answer to guide checking. Suggested rubric per item: Thesis and stand 20%, Use of chapter concepts (eudaimonia, the mean, phronesis, kabutihang panlahat) 40%, Evidence and examples 25%, Organization and clarity 15%. Suggested length: 300–500 words each.

1. As AI systems increasingly promise to optimize our choices, curate our entertainment, and even offer companionship, will they help this generation live the "good life" or quietly redefine it downward toward engineered pleasure and convenience? Argue a position using Aristotle's eudaimonia, the golden mean, and phronesis.

Short Model Answer

A strong answer takes a qualified stand. The optimistic case: AI can supply external goods (health, learning, freedom from drudgery) that give flourishing room, and — used with practical wisdom — can support virtuous activity. The cautionary case: AI optimized for engagement sells the vice of excess (endless curated pleasure) and can erode the effortful, reasoned activity eudaimonia requires, outsourcing the very deliberation (phronesis) through which we learn to live well; AI "companionship" may substitute a by-product for genuine relationship. The best essays argue the outcome is not fixed by the technology but by design and by the user's cultivated character and mean-finding — and that the danger is precisely that AI could redefine the good life downward by making the substitutes (pleasure, convenience) frictionless and the goods (struggle, mastery, real relationship) optional. They conclude that flourishing remains an achievement no system can deliver, so the decisive variable is examined human choice.

2. The "attention economy" treats human attention as a resource to be captured and sold. Drawing on Aristotle (the mean, phronesis), the Stoics (what is "up to us"), and the Filipino public good (kabutihang panlahat), argue how individuals and society should respond over the next decade so that technology serves the good life rather than feeding on it.

Short Model Answer

A strong answer works on two levels. Personal: cultivate the golden mean (purposeful use between deprivation and compulsion), Stoic discipline over what is "up to us" (our attention and responses, not others' opinions or the algorithm's pull), and practical wisdom about what deserves our scarcest resource. Social/structural: because the attention economy is engineered and systemic, individual virtue is necessary but insufficient; the public good (kabutihang panlahat) justifies collective action — design standards that respect attention, transparency about engagement optimization, data-privacy protections, and education (like STS itself). The best essays note the tension between market incentives (engagement = revenue) and the common good, and argue that a good society must sometimes constrain technologies that profit by corroding citizens' flourishing — while avoiding the deficiency-vice of blanket technophobia. They tie it back to the chapter's thesis that the good life is a shared, examined project.

3. Using the sugar case as a model, choose one contemporary "engineered pleasure" technology (e.g., ultra-processed food, mobile gaming, short-form video, or fast fashion) and analyze it across the three levels of the good life — personal flourishing, the public good, and sustainability. Propose one ethical, practically-wise response, personal or policy.

Short Model Answer

A strong answer mirrors the sugar analysis on a chosen case. Personal: identify the pleasure-vs-flourishing tension (e.g., short-form video's dopamine loops vs. sustained attention and real relationship) and apply the golden mean/temperance informed by evidence (Feynman: science informs but doesn't dictate). Public good: examine the community effects — mental-health costs, exploitation of the vulnerable (marketing to children), labor conditions (fast fashion's garment workers) — through a kapwa/kabutihang-panlahat lens. Sustainability: connect to environmental costs and the long-term view (fast fashion's waste and emissions; e-waste; the green-economy theme), noting that a good life cannot be bought at the community's or planet's expense. The proposed response should be concrete and practically wise — a personal practice (a self-imposed mean), a design change, or a policy (labeling, marketing limits, extended producer responsibility) — and justified by the chapter's framework rather than by slogan.
