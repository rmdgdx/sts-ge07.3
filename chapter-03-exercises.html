About These Exercises

This companion set to the Chapter 3 main lecture contains the higher-order-thinking assessment materials: a 20-item HOTS Chapter Test, a 20-item HOTS reinforcement assignment (take-home), and a 3-item futures essay. Each multiple-choice item has a click-to-reveal answer with explanation; the essays include short model answers and a suggested rubric. Refer to the main lecture (with its Self-Assessment quiz and references) for the content these exercises assess.

Chapter Test: 20-Item HOTS Multiple Choice

Higher-Order Thinking Skills test — items require analysis, evaluation, inference, and application, not recall. Click "Answer & Explanation" to reveal the key.

1. South Korea and the Philippines had similar income levels in the 1960s; today South Korea is far wealthier. Which inference BEST fits the chapter's argument?

  1. South Korea had more natural resources.
  2. Sustained national investment in education, research, and high-tech industry drove the divergence — development is a decision.
  3. Filipinos are less talented.
  4. The gap was pure luck.
Answer & Explanation

B. The comparison is the chapter's core evidence that deliberate S&T investment, not endowment, drives development. A is false (Korea is resource-poor; Singapore more so); C is contradicted by Filipino achievements; D ignores the identifiable policy choices.

2. The chapter insists the Philippines' S&T obstacles are "social and political, not scientific." The STRONGEST evidence for this is:

  1. Filipinos have produced no science.
  2. Filipino scientists demonstrably succeed (satellites, high-yield crops, test kits), so the limiting factor is funding and retention, not ability.
  3. Science is impossible in the tropics.
  4. Other countries never faced obstacles.
Answer & Explanation

B. Proven talent + poor outcomes implies the bottleneck is institutional/political. A and C are false; D is irrelevant and untrue.

3. PAGASA descends from Fr. Faura's 1860s typhoon service. This continuity BEST illustrates that:

  1. Philippine science never changes.
  2. A nation's physical environment durably shapes which sciences it invests in.
  3. Colonial science was useless.
  4. Weather forecasting is unimportant.
Answer & Explanation

B. Typhoons made meteorology a 150-year national priority — environment steering science. A overstates; C is contradicted by the life-saving service; D is false.

4. The Philippines has strong S&T laws yet low R&D spending and continuing brain drain. This gap BEST teaches that:

  1. Passing a law automatically solves a problem.
  2. Legislation without sustained funding and implementation does not, by itself, produce outcomes.
  3. Laws are irrelevant.
  4. Brain drain is unrelated to policy.
Answer & Explanation

B. The law-versus-outcome gap shows implementation and budget are decisive. A is the misconception exposed; C overcorrects; D ignores the opportunity-structure explanation.

5. A politician says brain drain proves Filipino scientists are "unpatriotic." Using the chapter's framing, the BEST rebuttal is:

  1. Agree — it is a moral failing of individuals.
  2. Migration follows opportunity structures (pay, equipment, careers); fix those and retention improves — it is a policy problem, not a character flaw.
  3. Scientists should be forced to stay.
  4. Brain drain does not exist.
Answer & Explanation

B. The STS framing locates cause in incentives and infrastructure, not virtue. A blames individuals for a structural problem; C is coercive and ignores the cause; D denies reality.

6. The Innovation Act aims to move the Philippines from technology "adopter" to "creator." Why does this distinction matter for development?

  1. Creators capture more of the economic value and gain self-reliance; adopters remain dependent and capture less.
  2. Adopting technology is always illegal.
  3. There is no difference.
  4. Creating technology is only about national pride.
Answer & Explanation

A. Value capture and reduced dependence are the strategic stakes. B is false; C denies the chapter's central concern; D understates the economic argument.

7. Lagundi and sambong were developed from folk remedies into approved medicines. This BEST models:

  1. That indigenous knowledge and formal science can validate and reinforce each other.
  2. That folk knowledge is always correct.
  3. That modern science should ignore tradition.
  4. That herbal medicine cannot be studied.
Answer & Explanation

A. The cases show integration: traditional leads, scientific validation. B overclaims (validation was needed); C and D are contradicted by the very examples.

8. Which best explains why the chapter treats DOST's PAGASA and PHIVOLCS as "survival infrastructure" rather than luxuries?

  1. They generate the most export revenue.
  2. In a nation facing ~20 cyclones a year and sitting on the Ring of Fire, forecasting and monitoring directly save lives and property.
  3. They are the oldest agencies.
  4. They are required by international law.
Answer & Explanation

B. Their value is tied to the archipelago's specific hazards. A is false; C and D are not the reason given.

9. Underinvestment is called "the root cause from which many other problems grow." The BEST reasoning is that low R&D funding:

  1. Directly increases typhoons.
  2. Starves labs and salaries, which worsens brain drain, dependence, and the commercialization gap.
  3. Has no downstream effects.
  4. Only affects universities.
Answer & Explanation

B. Funding is upstream of retention, capacity, and commercialization. A is absurd; C and D understate the cascade.

10. The "commercialization gap" ("valley of death") refers to:

  1. Scientists dying young.
  2. Good research failing to become products, jobs, or exports due to weak university–government–industry links.
  3. A shortage of researchers.
  4. The gap between two laboratories.
Answer & Explanation

B. It is the failure to translate research into market impact. The others misread the term.

11. Diwata-1 was built by Filipino engineers. Beyond its technical function, the chapter values it MOST as:

  1. A source of immediate profit.
  2. Evidence of homegrown capability that builds national confidence and reduces dependence.
  3. A replacement for PAGASA.
  4. A purely symbolic object with no use.
Answer & Explanation

B. The chapter frames national achievements as capability plus confidence. A is not the point; C is false; D ignores its real applications (agriculture, disaster, weather).

12. Using Sen's view of development, which outcome counts as genuine "development" even if GDP barely moves?

  1. A luxury mall opens in Manila.
  2. Rural clinics gain reliable electricity and vaccines, extending healthy life and expanding people's real capabilities.
  3. The stock index rises for a week.
  4. A few firms post record profits.
Answer & Explanation

B. Sen defines development as expanded capabilities and freedoms, not mere output. The others are growth or wealth signals without necessarily expanding people's real freedoms.

13. Why does the chapter call indigenous knowledge part of the "national scientific inheritance" rather than mere folklore?

  1. Because it is old.
  2. Because practices like the terraces and ethnomedicine are tested, functional solutions — engineering and empirical knowledge, not superstition.
  3. Because the government mandates it.
  4. Because it is written down.
Answer & Explanation

B. The criterion is function and testing, not age (A), decree (C), or medium (D — much IKS is oral).

14. A group proposes to patent a medicine derived from an indigenous community's plant knowledge without sharing benefits. The BEST STS critique is:

  1. It is fine; discovery belongs to whoever patents it.
  2. It risks biopiracy — extracting value from a community's knowledge without credit or fair benefit-sharing.
  3. Indigenous knowledge has no value.
  4. Patents are always illegal.
Answer & Explanation

B. The chapter flags biopiracy and fair benefit-sharing as the ethical issue. A ignores the injustice; C and D are false.

15. The pace of S&T laws quickened around 2018–2019 (Balik Scientist, Innovation, Startup, Space Acts). This clustering BEST suggests:

  1. Random legislative activity.
  2. A deliberate national push to catch up and build innovation capacity in a technological century.
  3. That earlier decades had no science policy.
  4. That the laws were identical.
Answer & Explanation

B. The cluster reads as a coordinated catch-up effort. A ignores the coherence; C is false (1958, 1987, 1997); D is wrong (distinct laws).

16. Which allocation best reflects the chapter's claim that underinvestment is the ROOT problem?

  1. Spend only on national celebrations of science.
  2. Raise sustained R&D funding toward the ~1% benchmark, enabling salaries, scholarships, and labs that address downstream problems.
  3. Ban technology imports outright.
  4. Cut the DOST budget to force efficiency.
Answer & Explanation

B. Addressing the root (funding) enables solutions to retention, capacity, and commercialization. A is symbolic; C is unworkable and harmful; D worsens the root cause.

17. The Green Revolution centered at IRRI (Los Baños) fits which Chapter 1 pattern applied to nation-building?

  1. Technology has only good effects.
  2. "Need precedes invention": the social need to feed a growing population drove high-yield crop science, with real benefits and real chemical-dependence costs.
  3. Science is value-neutral.
  4. Rice research had no downside.
Answer & Explanation

B. It exemplifies need-driven science with a double-edged outcome. A and D ignore the costs; C is not the pattern in question.

18. A student argues the Philippines should simply buy all its technology abroad instead of funding costly local R&D. The BEST counter, using the chapter, is:

  1. Importing is always cheaper and better, so agree.
  2. Pure dependence forfeits value capture, resilience, and the ability to adapt technology to local needs (typhoons, crops, languages) — self-reliance matters.
  3. Local R&D never works.
  4. Technology cannot be imported.
Answer & Explanation

B. The self-reliance argument: dependence is strategically costly and poorly fitted to local conditions. A ignores those costs; C is false; D is absurd.

19. The digital divide is a nation-building concern MOST because:

  1. Cities deserve more resources.
  2. Development that reaches only some Filipinos is incomplete; concentrating S&T in Metro Manila leaves regions behind.
  3. Rural areas do not need technology.
  4. Internet access is a luxury.
Answer & Explanation

B. Equitable reach is intrinsic to genuine nation-building. A, C, and D contradict the chapter's inclusive view of development.

20. Taking the whole chapter, which statement is the MOST defensible thesis about Philippine S&T?

  1. The Philippines lacks the talent to develop through science.
  2. The Philippines has proven talent and institutions but is held back by choices — funding, retention, deployment — that it has the power to change.
  3. Development happens automatically once laws are passed.
  4. Indigenous knowledge and modern science are incompatible.
Answer & Explanation

B. It integrates the chapter's threads (talent + institutions + reversible political choices). A is refuted by achievements; C ignores the law-outcome gap; D is contradicted by lagundi/sambong.

Reinforcement Exercise (Assignment): 20-Item HOTS Multiple Choice

Take-home reinforcement. Same HOTS orientation, new angles on the chapter. Keys with explanations are collapsible for self-checking or teacher-only printing.

1. Singapore has almost no natural resources yet became wealthy through S&T. The BEST lesson for the Philippines is:

  1. Resource wealth is necessary for development.
  2. Deliberate investment in human capital and technology can substitute for natural endowments.
  3. Small countries cannot develop.
  4. Development requires luck.
Answer & Explanation

B. Singapore proves capability can be built rather than inherited. A is refuted by the case; C and D contradict it.

2. Which correctly pairs an agency with its core function?

  1. PHIVOLCS → weather forecasting.
  2. PAGASA → typhoons and weather; PHIVOLCS → volcanoes and earthquakes.
  3. PhilSA → herbal medicine.
  4. NEDA → building satellites.
Answer & Explanation

B. Correct pairing. A swaps PHIVOLCS's role; PhilSA is space; NEDA is socioeconomic planning, not satellite construction.

3. A "foresight" document like Pagtanaw 2050 differs from a 6-year plan chiefly because it:

  1. Is legally binding on all citizens.
  2. Treats the long-term future as something to be deliberately shaped, setting direction decades ahead.
  3. Predicts the future with certainty.
  4. Replaces the Constitution.
Answer & Explanation

B. Foresight sets long-range direction, not certainty (C) or law (A, D).

4. The Magna Carta for Scientists (RA 8439) addresses brain drain by targeting which cause?

  1. The weather.
  2. Poor pay and benefits that push talent abroad — improving retention incentives at home.
  3. A lack of universities.
  4. Excess funding.
Answer & Explanation

B. The law improves compensation to retain researchers — an opportunity-structure fix. The others are not its mechanism.

5. Which is the clearest example of science building "national confidence," as the chapter uses the phrase?

  1. Importing a foreign-made satellite.
  2. Filipino engineers designing and launching Diwata-1, proving Filipinos can do it themselves.
  3. Buying medicine from abroad.
  4. Ranking universities.
Answer & Explanation

B. Homegrown achievement builds the belief in local capability. A and C are dependence; D is unrelated.

6. The 1987 Constitution's S&T clause is best described in STS terms as:

  1. A purely symbolic sentence with no consequence.
  2. A "social technology" — an engineered commitment meant to steer national priorities toward research and innovation.
  3. A scientific discovery.
  4. A ban on technology.
Answer & Explanation

B. Like law generally, it is designed social machinery to direct behavior and resources. A understates its intent; C and D misclassify it.

7. If a law is strong but chronically unfunded, the MOST accurate conclusion is:

  1. The problem is solved because the law exists.
  2. Policy intent and policy outcome diverge without budget and implementation — the binding constraint is resources, not legislation.
  3. The law caused the underfunding.
  4. Funding is irrelevant to science.
Answer & Explanation

B. The chapter's law-outcome gap. A is the misconception; C confuses correlation; D is false.

8. The Ifugao rice terraces are cited in a nation-building chapter chiefly to argue that:

  1. Only modern labs produce real science.
  2. Filipino scientific and engineering capability is an ancient, indigenous inheritance, not a foreign import.
  3. Terraces are merely scenic.
  4. Agriculture is unimportant.
Answer & Explanation

B. They ground national scientific identity in indigenous engineering. A contradicts the chapter; C and D dismiss their substance.

9. A region far from Manila lacks laboratories and fast internet. Framed by the chapter, this is a failure of:

  1. Individual effort.
  2. Equitable distribution — the digital divide undermining inclusive nation-building.
  3. The weather.
  4. Nothing; concentration is ideal.
Answer & Explanation

B. Uneven access is a distributional/nation-building failure. A blames individuals; C is irrelevant; D contradicts inclusive development.

10. Which best captures why "development" ≠ "GDP growth" in this chapter?

  1. They are identical.
  2. Development expands people's real capabilities and freedoms (health, education, agency), which growth alone may not deliver.
  3. GDP is always negative.
  4. Development ignores the economy entirely.
Answer & Explanation

B. Sen's capabilities view. A collapses the distinction; C is false; D overcorrects.

11. Project NOAH built hazard maps and early-warning systems. This exemplifies S&T serving nation-building through:

  1. Export revenue.
  2. Resilience — reducing the human and economic toll of the nation's greatest vulnerability, disasters.
  3. National pride only.
  4. Entertainment.
Answer & Explanation

B. Disaster science builds resilience, one of the four development channels. The others miss its purpose.

12. During COVID-19, locally-developed test kits appeared quickly. The BEST interpretation is:

  1. The kits came from nowhere.
  2. They were the payoff of decades of quiet capacity-building (labs, trained scientists), showing why sustained investment matters.
  3. Local science is useless.
  4. Imports would always have been better.
Answer & Explanation

B. Crisis revealed the value of pre-built capacity. A ignores the foundations; C and D are contradicted by the achievement.

13. Which policy pairing correctly matches a problem to a targeted response?

  1. Brain drain → Space Act.
  2. Brain drain → Balik Scientist Act; technology dependence → Innovation Act.
  3. Disasters → Data Privacy Act.
  4. Innovation gap → PhilSA.
Answer & Explanation

B. Correct problem→policy mapping. The others mismatch the instruments.

14. The chapter's comparison to South Korea is MOST vulnerable to which fair objection?

  1. South Korea never invested in science.
  2. Historical, geopolitical, and aid contexts differed (e.g., Cold War support, land reform), so "just choose to invest" oversimplifies.
  3. The Philippines has no scientists.
  4. Comparisons are never useful.
Answer & Explanation

B. A sophisticated answer notes confounding contexts while keeping the core lesson. A is false; C is refuted; D is anti-analytical.

15. Fe del Mundo's electricity-free incubator best exemplifies which principle of successful Filipino science?

  1. Copying foreign designs exactly.
  2. Adapting technology to local realities (rural clinics without power).
  3. Prioritizing prestige over use.
  4. Avoiding medicine.
Answer & Explanation

B. Local adaptation is the pattern the chapter highlights. A, C, and D contradict it.

16. Which reasoning best defends funding "unglamorous" disaster science over a flashier prestige project?

  1. Prestige projects never help.
  2. In a hazard-prone archipelago, forecasting and hazard mapping save the most lives per peso — matching investment to the nation's actual risks.
  3. Disaster science is cheap so quality does not matter.
  4. Only prestige builds nations.
Answer & Explanation

B. Fit investment to real vulnerabilities. A overgeneralizes; C is a non sequitur; D is the opposite of the chapter's resilience argument.

17. Integrating indigenous knowledge with formal science is described as a "distinctively Filipino path." The BEST reason is that it:

  1. Rejects all modern science.
  2. Combines locally-adapted, community-tested solutions with scientific validation, grounding development in Filipino identity.
  3. Replaces laboratories with folklore.
  4. Is required by foreign donors.
Answer & Explanation

B. Integration (not replacement) is the point. A and C misstate it; D invents a motive.

18. A budget plan funds scholarships and rural labs but freezes researcher salaries. The chapter predicts the LIKELY result:

  1. Brain drain ends immediately.
  2. Trained talent may still leave for better pay abroad, blunting the gains — retention needs competitive compensation too.
  3. Salaries are irrelevant to retention.
  4. Rural labs cause emigration.
Answer & Explanation

B. Opportunity structures include pay; ignoring it undercuts retention. A is optimistic fantasy; C contradicts the Magna Carta's logic; D is illogical.

19. Which is the STRONGEST argument that S&T self-reliance supports national sovereignty?

  1. Self-reliance means total isolation.
  2. A nation that can secure its own food, energy, data, and disaster response depends less on others and withstands shocks and pressure better.
  3. Sovereignty has nothing to do with technology.
  4. Importing everything maximizes sovereignty.
Answer & Explanation

B. Capability reduces vulnerability and dependence. A misdefines self-reliance (which allows exchange); C and D are backwards.

20. Synthesizing the chapter, "development is a decision" is BEST understood to mean:

  1. Development happens by accident.
  2. A nation's scientific progress results from deliberate, reversible choices about investment, retention, and deployment — not from fate or endowment.
  3. Only rich nations can decide to develop.
  4. Decisions do not affect outcomes.
Answer & Explanation

B. The thesis emphasizes agency and reversibility of policy choices. A and D deny agency; C is refuted by Singapore/South Korea starting poor.

Essay: The Future of Chapter 3's Topics (3 Items)

Each item includes a short model answer to guide checking. Suggested rubric per item: Thesis and stand 20%, Use of chapter concepts 40%, Evidence and examples 25%, Organization and clarity 15%. Suggested length: 300–500 words each.

1. The chapter argues "development is a decision." Design a realistic 10-year plan to move the Philippines meaningfully toward the 1%-of-GDP R&D benchmark. Identify your top three priorities, justify them using the chapter's challenges, and anticipate one major political obstacle.

Short Model Answer

A strong answer sets a clear direction and reasons from the chapter's five challenges. Priorities might be: (1) raise sustained R&D funding on a legislated, multi-year schedule toward ~1% of GDP — the root-cause fix that enables everything else; (2) attack brain drain at its source by making researcher pay, equipment, and career paths competitive (strengthening the Magna Carta and Balik Scientist Program), since scholarships without retention just train talent for export; (3) close the commercialization gap by funding university–industry linkages and the Startup/Innovation Act ecosystem so research becomes products, jobs, and exports. Justification ties each to a named challenge (underinvestment, brain drain, commercialization gap). The anticipated obstacle is political: R&D pays off beyond a single electoral cycle, so it competes poorly against short-term spending — the essay should propose a safeguard (a legislated funding floor, an independent innovation council, or earmarked revenue) to protect it. The best essays acknowledge trade-offs and note that "a decision" must be a sustained one.

2. As AI and automation reshape the global economy, the Philippines' large BPO and services sector is both an asset and a vulnerability. Using the chapter's ideas of self-reliance, innovation, and the "adopter vs. creator" distinction, argue how the country should position its S&T strategy for the next two decades.

Short Model Answer

A strong answer confronts the double edge: AI could automate routine BPO tasks the economy depends on, yet also enables new higher-value work if the country moves up the value chain. Applying the chapter: pure "adopter" status (importing and merely using foreign AI) leaves the Philippines capturing little value and exposed to disruption; the "creator" path — building domestic AI research (NAICRI, the AI Roadmap), data infrastructure, and AI-literate graduates — captures more value and builds self-reliance. Recommendations should include: reskilling the BPO workforce toward AI-augmented and creative roles; investing in local-language and locally-relevant AI (agriculture, disaster, health) where global firms won't; and pairing this with governance (data privacy, AI regulation) so adoption is safe and sovereign. The strongest essays connect to Chapter 1's Luddite lesson — plan for displaced workers, don't just celebrate the technology — and to the chapter's thesis that the outcome depends on deliberate national choices, not on the technology alone.

3. Indigenous knowledge (rice terraces, ethnomedicine, sustainable resource management) is increasingly valued for national development and climate resilience. Evaluate how the Philippines should integrate indigenous knowledge systems with formal science over the coming decades — including how to do so ethically and avoid exploitation.

Short Model Answer

A strong answer argues for genuine integration rather than either dismissal or romanticization. The development case: ethnomedicine (lagundi, sambong) can yield affordable medicines; terrace hydraulics and traditional resource-management (muyong, zanjera) offer tested models for climate adaptation and sustainability in a disaster-prone nation. The ethical case is essential: formal study of indigenous knowledge risks biopiracy — extracting value without credit or fair benefit-sharing — so integration must include free and informed consent, recognition of community intellectual-property rights, and equitable benefit-sharing with the communities that hold the knowledge. Institutionally, this means partnerships (not extraction) between DOST/universities and indigenous communities, documentation that communities control, and legal protection of IKS. The best essays tie this to the chapter's identity argument — that recognizing Filipino knowledge builds national confidence — while insisting that justice to the source communities is a precondition, not an afterthought, and note that indigenous sustainability practices are increasingly relevant to the climate challenges of Week 16.
