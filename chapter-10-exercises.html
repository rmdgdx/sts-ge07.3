About These Exercises

This companion set to the Chapter 10 main lecture contains the higher-order-thinking assessment materials: a 20-item HOTS Chapter Test, a 20-item HOTS reinforcement assignment (take-home), and a 3-item futures essay. Chapter Test: 20-Item HOTS Multiple Choice

Higher-Order Thinking Skills test — items require analysis, evaluation, inference, and application, not recall. Click "Answer & Explanation" to reveal the key.

1. Gold is inert and yellow in bulk but reactive and reddish at the nanoscale. This BEST illustrates why:

  1. Nanotechnology is impossible.
  2. Materials behave differently at the nanoscale, which is the source of both nanotech's usefulness and its novel risks.
  3. Gold is not a metal.
  4. Size never affects properties.
Answer & Explanation

B. Scale-dependent behavior is the core insight. A and C are false; D is the opposite.

2. The chapter calls nanotech a "convergent and enabling technology." The MAIN governance challenge this creates is that:

  1. It does only one thing, so it is easy to regulate.
  2. Because it is embedded invisibly in thousands of products, its benefits and risks are both diffuse and hard to track.
  3. It has no applications.
  4. It is banned everywhere.
Answer & Explanation

B. Diffuse, invisible embedding complicates governance. A, C, D are false.

3. The property that makes nanoparticles useful for drug delivery (tiny size, ability to cross barriers) is ALSO the source of which concern?

  1. The nano-divide.
  2. Nanotoxicology — the same penetration could make them hazardous to cells and ecosystems in ways not fully known.
  3. Grey goo.
  4. Surveillance.
Answer & Explanation

B. Usefulness and toxicity share the same root property. The others are different nanoethics concerns.

4. The "regulatory gap" in nanoethics arises because:

  1. Nanomaterials are identical to their bulk forms.
  2. Existing safety rules were written for bulk chemicals and may not apply to nanomaterials that behave differently.
  3. There are no regulators.
  4. Nanotech is illegal.
Answer & Explanation

B. Rules written for bulk materials misfit the nanoscale. A contradicts the science; C and D are false.

5. A patient's blood stem cells are edited outside the body and returned, curing their sickle-cell disease. This is:

  1. Germline editing.
  2. Somatic editing — affecting only the individual, not their descendants.
  3. Enhancement.
  4. Reproductive cloning.
Answer & Explanation

B. Editing body cells (not eggs/sperm/embryos) is somatic. A, C, D misclassify it.

6. Why is the somatic/germline distinction called the "ethical hinge" of gene editing?

  1. Because somatic editing is more dangerous.
  2. Because germline editing affects all descendants and the human gene pool permanently, and those affected cannot consent — while somatic editing stops with the patient.
  3. Because they are the same thing.
  4. Because neither is possible.
Answer & Explanation

B. Heritability + non-consent is what raises germline's stakes. A is backwards; C and D are false.

7. Casgevy (2023) is significant for this chapter chiefly because it:

  1. Is a germline enhancement.
  2. Is the first approved CRISPR therapy — a responsible, somatic cure demonstrating gene editing has become real medicine.
  3. Was created secretly.
  4. Alters patients' children.
Answer & Explanation

B. It is the promise realized: somatic, approved, curative. A, C, D describe the opposite (He Jiankui-type) case.

8. The He Jiankui affair and Casgevy differ from each other precisely along which line?

  1. Cheap vs. expensive.
  2. Germline (He Jiankui, reckless, heritable) vs. somatic (Casgevy, responsible, non-heritable).
  3. Chinese vs. American.
  4. Old vs. new.
Answer & Explanation

B. The somatic/germline line is exactly what separates them. The others are irrelevant.

9. Even granting He Jiankui's stated good intentions, the universal condemnation shows that:

  1. Good intentions justify any action.
  2. What technology can do does not settle what we should do — consent, safety, necessity, heritability, and process all matter.
  3. HIV resistance is unimportant.
  4. Germline editing is always safe.
Answer & Explanation

B. The is/ought and process lessons. A is the error exposed; C and D are false.

10. The "therapy-vs-enhancement" problem in germline editing is that:

  1. Therapy and enhancement are identical.
  2. Once editing to prevent disease is permitted, drawing a stable line against editing for traits like height or intelligence becomes genuinely difficult.
  3. Enhancement is impossible.
  4. Therapy is unethical.
Answer & Explanation

B. The slippery slope from therapy to enhancement is the concern. A, C, D misstate it.

11. The "genetic divide"/"designer babies" worry about germline enhancement is that it could:

  1. Cure all disease equally for everyone.
  2. Create a permanent biological caste system if available only to the wealthy — a new, market-driven eugenics.
  3. Make everyone genetically identical.
  4. Eliminate inequality.
Answer & Explanation

B. Enhancement-driven biological inequality is the fear (Ch. 7). A, C, D are opposite or false.

12. The global consensus against heritable human editing is described as an example of:

  1. Reckless acceleration.
  2. Humanity collectively drawing a line and choosing not (yet) to use a power it possesses — relinquishment applied to our own genome.
  3. Scientism.
  4. Technological determinism.
Answer & Explanation

B. It is deliberate non-use (Ch. 7's relinquishment). The others mischaracterize it.

13. An anti-vaccine post claims "mRNA vaccines rewrite your DNA." The chapter's correction is that:

  1. This is true.
  2. mRNA instructs cells to make a viral fragment and does NOT enter or alter DNA — a case where misinformation had real public-health consequences.
  3. Vaccines contain no mRNA.
  4. DNA does not exist.
Answer & Explanation

B. mRNA doesn't alter DNA; the claim is Ch. 8 misinformation. A is false; C and D are absurd.

14. mRNA vaccines were developed in under a year "only because of decades of prior basic research." The BEST policy lesson is:

  1. Basic research is a waste.
  2. Sustained long-term research investment builds the platforms that enable rapid response to future emergencies — preparedness is decided years in advance.
  3. Science should fund only immediate payoffs.
  4. Pandemics cannot be prepared for.
Answer & Explanation

B. The payoff-of-patience lesson (Ch. 3). A and C are the opposite; D is false.

15. That mRNA vaccines reached rich nations first and faced misinformation shows that:

  1. Technology's impact is independent of society.
  2. Society shapes a technology's outcomes — through equity of access and the information environment — not just the science.
  3. Vaccines don't work.
  4. Misinformation is harmless.
Answer & Explanation

B. Co-production: social choices shape outcomes. A, C, D are false.

16. The development of induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) is presented as an example of:

  1. Science ignoring ethics.
  2. Ethical concern (about embryo use) steering scientists toward a more acceptable technical solution — science and society co-producing a path.
  3. A failure of research.
  4. Germline editing.
Answer & Explanation

B. Ethics reshaped the research direction. A is the opposite; C and D are false.

17. Unproven, expensive "stem-cell therapy" clinics illustrate which cross-chapter danger?

  1. That stem cells have no promise.
  2. That promising science attracts predatory hype (Ch. 8), so vulnerable patients need protection through regulation and scientific literacy.
  3. That regulation is unnecessary.
  4. That all stem-cell medicine is fake.
Answer & Explanation

B. Hype exploiting desperate patients links to Ch. 8. A and D overstate; C is the opposite of the lesson.

18. Which statement BEST reflects the chapter's balanced stance on these technologies?

  1. They are miracles; deploy them all with no limits.
  2. They are menaces; ban them all.
  3. They offer extraordinary benefits and profound risks; proceed with responsible innovation, ethics, and governance, refusing both hype and fear.
  4. They are irrelevant to society.
Answer & Explanation

C. The two-sided, responsible-innovation stance. A and B are the extremes; D is false.

19. A student argues, "If we can edit the germline to prevent Huntington's, we clearly should, full stop." The chapter's BEST response is:

  1. Fully agree; no further thought needed.
  2. The good of preventing disease is real, but must be weighed against irreversibility, the consent of future generations, the therapy-to-enhancement slope, inequality, and the lack of consensus/governance — "can" does not settle "should."
  3. Huntington's is not serious.
  4. Germline editing is impossible.
Answer & Explanation

B. It holds the genuine good against the serious cautions. A ignores the stakes; C and D are false.

20. Synthesizing the chapter, its unifying conviction is that our growing power to remake matter and life:

  1. Makes ethics and governance obsolete.
  2. Makes wisdom, ethics, and governance MORE essential, because these technologies can heal or entrench inequality and cross irreversible lines — so the future must be chosen, not merely built.
  3. Guarantees a good outcome automatically.
  4. Means we should never use any of them.
Answer & Explanation

B. Greater power raises the stakes of wise choice. A and C are complacent; D is reflexive technophobia.

Essay: The Future of Chapter 10's Topics (3 Items)

Each item includes a short model answer to guide checking. Suggested rubric per item: Thesis and stand 20%, Use of chapter concepts (somatic/germline, therapy/enhancement, precaution) 40%, Evidence and examples 25%, Organization and clarity 15%. Suggested length: 300–500 words each.

1. Should heritable (germline) human genome editing ever be permitted? Argue a clear position for the coming decades, using the somatic/germline distinction, the therapy-vs-enhancement problem, the He Jiankui case, the consent and inequality arguments, and the current global consensus.

Short Model Answer

A strong answer takes a clear, defensible stand and reasons with the chapter's framework. A common well-argued position: permit and advance somatic therapy (Casgevy-style cures) enthusiastically, but maintain a moratorium on heritable germline editing for now — because it is currently unsafe (off-target risks), irreversible, affects future people who cannot consent, slides hard from therapy toward enhancement, threatens a genetic divide and a market-driven eugenics, and lacks the broad societal consensus and governance such a species-altering step demands. The He Jiankui affair is the cautionary anchor: it proves the capability exists and can be abused, and that good intentions do not justify crossing the line. The best essays engage the strongest counter-argument honestly — that germline editing could someday eliminate devastating diseases like Huntington's — and respond that even a real good must be weighed against irreversibility and consent, and that the burden of proof (precautionary principle) lies on demonstrating safety, equity, and consensus first. They conclude with the course's theme: "can" does not settle "should," and this is the clearest case where the future must be deliberately chosen.

2. mRNA vaccines emerged rapidly during COVID-19 only because of decades of prior basic research, yet were distributed unequally and undermined by misinformation. Using this case, argue how the Philippines should invest in and govern biomedical science and pandemic preparedness over the next decade.

Short Model Answer

A strong answer draws the triple lesson: (1) sustained basic-research funding is preparedness — the mRNA platform existed in 2020 only because of decades of unglamorous work, so the Philippines should raise and protect long-term R&D investment (Ch. 3) rather than funding only immediate payoffs; (2) equity matters — vaccine nationalism left poorer nations last, so the country should invest in local manufacturing/biotech capacity and regional cooperation to reduce dependence; (3) the information environment shapes outcomes — misinformation (that mRNA "alters DNA") cost lives, so pandemic preparedness must include information literacy and public trust-building (Ch. 8). The best essays connect these to the national S&T system (DOST, the AI/biotech roadmap, genomic-surveillance capacity built during COVID-19), argue that preparedness is a deliberate decision made years in advance, and note the One Health framing (Ch. 9) that ties human, animal, and environmental health to pandemic risk.

3. Nanotechnology is an invisible, enabling technology already embedded in thousands of products, with benefits and risks that are both real and diffuse. Argue how society should govern it responsibly over the coming decades — balancing innovation, the precautionary principle, equity, and the challenge that its effects are hard to track.

Short Model Answer

A strong answer resists both hype and fear, applying the nanoethics balance. It should acknowledge nanotech's vast, diffuse benefits (medicine, energy, materials, water) and its equally diffuse risks (nanotoxicology, the regulatory gap, the nano-divide, invisible surveillance), and argue for responsible innovation rather than blanket acceleration or prohibition. Concrete governance proposals: invest in nanotoxicology and lifecycle research to close knowledge gaps; update safety regulation to reflect that nanomaterials behave differently from bulk forms (the regulatory gap); require transparency/labeling of engineered nanomaterials in consumer products so the public can make informed choices; ensure equitable access so benefits do not accrue only to the rich (the nano-divide, linking to Ch. 3); and apply proportionate precaution — stronger scrutiny where exposure and novelty are highest. The best essays note the special difficulty that nanotech's invisibility and ubiquity make tracking and governance uniquely hard, and tie the recommendation to the course theme that powerful new technologies make wisdom and governance more essential, not less — and, via water purification and efficient solar cells, to the sustainability and climate themes of the next chapter.
