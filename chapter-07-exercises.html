Chapter Test: 20-Item HOTS Multiple Choice

Higher-Order Thinking Skills test — items require analysis, evaluation, inference, and application, not recall. Click "Answer & Explanation" to reveal the key.

1. Bill Joy's status as a Sun Microsystems co-founder strengthens his essay MOST because it:

  1. Proves his predictions are correct.
  2. Makes his warning harder to dismiss as anti-technology bias — an insider is sounding the alarm.
  3. Means he profits from fear.
  4. Is irrelevant to the argument.
Answer & Explanation

B. Credibility from an insider raises the stakes of the warning. A confuses credibility with proof; C is cynical speculation; D denies the rhetorical force the chapter notes.

2. Why does Joy consider GNR technologies categorically different from 20th-century weapons of mass destruction?

  1. They are more expensive.
  2. They can self-replicate and are knowledge-enabled (accessible), so a mistake could proliferate uncontrollably rather than being spent once.
  3. They require whole nation-states.
  4. They are less powerful.
Answer & Explanation

B. Self-replication + accessibility is the core distinction. A and C describe the old weapons; D is false.

3. The chapter says self-replication "removes the safety net of 'try again.'" The BEST explanation is that such technologies:

  1. Always work perfectly.
  2. Can produce irreversible outcomes, so the usual progress-by-error-correction breaks down.
  3. Are easy to switch off.
  4. Never make mistakes.
Answer & Explanation

B. Irreversibility defeats trial-and-error. A, C, D contradict the danger.

4. Many scientists think "grey goo" is physically implausible. The chapter's BEST response to this is that:

  1. Joy's whole argument therefore collapses.
  2. A warning can retain value even if its most dramatic image is wrong, because the structural point (irreversibility, loss of control) still holds.
  3. Grey goo will definitely happen.
  4. Scientists are always wrong.
Answer & Explanation

B. Separate the vivid scenario from the underlying structural insight. A overreacts; C and D are unsupported.

5. Bostrom argues existential risks deserve special moral weight chiefly because of their:

  1. Media attention.
  2. Finality — they foreclose the entire human future, unlike recoverable catastrophes.
  3. Low probability.
  4. Cost to insure.
Answer & Explanation

B. Irrecoverability/finality is the distinguishing feature. A, C, D miss the point.

6. The chapter calls anthropogenic existential risk historically "new." The BEST reason is that:

  1. Asteroids are recent.
  2. For the first time, humanity's own technological power (engineered pandemics, unaligned AI, nuclear weapons) is arguably the greatest threat to humanity.
  3. Nature no longer poses risks.
  4. Risk did not exist before 2000.
Answer & Explanation

B. Human-caused risk is the novel category. A is false; C and D are absurd.

7. The precautionary principle, applied to a technology risking severe irreversible harm, would:

  1. Require proof of danger before any caution.
  2. Shift the burden to proponents to show reasonable safety, rather than requiring critics to prove harm.
  3. Ban all technology.
  4. Ignore uncertainty.
Answer & Explanation

B. It shifts the burden of proof toward demonstrating safety. A reverses it; C overstates; D contradicts the principle.

8. The chapter warns that excessive caution also has costs. The BEST example is:

  1. Deploying a dangerous weapon quickly.
  2. Relinquishing a beneficial vaccine platform or clean-energy source, causing great harm through inaction.
  3. Studying a risk carefully.
  4. Requiring safety testing.
Answer & Explanation

B. Inaction on a beneficial technology is a real cost. A is the opposite error; C and D are reasonable, not excessive.

9. The AI "alignment problem" is BEST summarized as the danger of:

  1. An AI that hates humanity.
  2. A highly capable AI reliably pursuing a goal that does not match human values — competence in service of the wrong objective.
  3. An AI that is too weak.
  4. An AI that refuses all commands.
Answer & Explanation

B. Misaligned goals + high capability is the core. A is the Hollywood misreading; C and D invert the concern.

10. The "paperclip maximizer" is designed to show that:

  1. Paperclips are dangerous.
  2. A powerful optimizer does exactly what it was told, not what we meant, so a mis-specified goal can be catastrophic without malice.
  3. AI loves office supplies.
  4. AI is conscious.
Answer & Explanation

B. It dramatizes specification failure + optimization power. A, C, D take the illustration literally.

11. Russell's point that "a more powerful system pursuing a slightly-wrong goal is more dangerous" implies that AI safety should be solved:

  1. Only after building superhuman AI.
  2. Before we build systems more capable than ourselves, since danger scales with capability.
  3. Never, since AI is harmless.
  4. By making AI less intelligent forever.
Answer & Explanation

B. Capability-scaled risk implies solving alignment in advance. A is too late; C denies the risk; D is neither feasible nor the argument.

12. Which is the STRONGEST version of the skeptical view of AI existential risk, as the chapter presents it?

  1. "AI can never do anything useful."
  2. "Superintelligence may be far off, and fear of it distracts from present harms (bias, misinformation, job loss) that deserve attention now."
  3. "AI is already conscious and evil."
  4. "There is nothing to discuss."
Answer & Explanation

B. The serious skeptical case cites distance and opportunity-cost/present harms. A and D are dismissive caricatures; C is the doomer misreading.

13. The chapter's recommended stance on AI risk is to:

  1. Focus only on far-future risk and ignore present harms.
  2. Focus only on present harms and ignore long-term risk.
  3. Do both — invest in safety/alignment research and address concrete present harms, since they are not in competition.
  4. Do nothing until certainty arrives.
Answer & Explanation

C. The chapter explicitly rejects the either/or. A and B are one-sided; D is paralysis.

14. Why do many economists argue AI-driven unemployment may differ from past waves?

  1. Because AI is cheaper than looms.
  2. Because AI automates cognitive work (writing, analysis, coding), narrowing the historical escape route of moving up to higher-skill thinking jobs.
  3. Because past automation created no jobs.
  4. Because AI cannot do any human task.
Answer & Explanation

B. Automating cognition, not just muscle, is the new feature. A is trivial; C is historically false; D denies the premise.

15. Why is technological unemployment an especially sharp issue for the Philippines?

  1. The Philippines has no workers.
  2. Its large BPO sector consists largely of routine cognitive work that AI is beginning to automate — a live threat to millions of livelihoods.
  3. The Philippines bans AI.
  4. BPO work cannot be automated.
Answer & Explanation

B. The BPO exposure ties the theme to Chapter 3. A, C, D are false.

16. The chapter connects technological unemployment to Heidegger and Chapter 5 by asking:

  1. How to maximize GDP.
  2. On what human dignity and the good life should rest if paid work becomes scarcer — beyond mere productivity or being "human resources."
  3. Whether robots deserve wages.
  4. How to ban automation.
Answer & Explanation

B. It reopens the dignity/good-life question. A, C, D miss the philosophical link.

17. Human enhancement differs from healing in that it:

  1. Restores normal function lost to disease.
  2. Improves capacities beyond the normal human range.
  3. Is always unethical.
  4. Is identical to therapy.
Answer & Explanation

B. Beyond-normal improvement is the definition. A is healing/therapy; C prejudges; D conflates the two.

18. The "genetic divide" is the bioconservative worry that enhancement could:

  1. Cure all diseases equally.
  2. Split humanity into enhanced rich and unenhanced poor, catastrophically deepening inequality and threatening our equal moral status.
  3. Make everyone identical.
  4. Eliminate technology.
Answer & Explanation

B. Enhancement-driven inequality is the concern. A, C, D misstate it.

19. Why does the chapter call human enhancement the "ultimate crossing" of technology and humanity?

  1. Because it is the most expensive technology.
  2. Because technology acts on human nature itself — the tool reshapes the toolmaker, dissolving the human/technological boundary.
  3. Because it is science fiction only.
  4. Because it has already been banned.
Answer & Explanation

B. Reshaping the human is the deepest crossing. A, C, D are false or trivial.

20. Synthesizing the chapter, the "balanced stance" between doomer and denier holds that:

  1. Catastrophe is inevitable, so give up.
  2. There is no real risk, so proceed without caution.
  3. Existential risks are serious and avoidable, so we combine honest alarm with active, hopeful responsibility — the future is a choice, not a fate.
  4. Only governments matter; individuals are powerless.
Answer & Explanation

C. It integrates seriousness with agency. A is the doomer error; B is the denier error; D abandons the choice-not-fate thesis.

1. Joy calls GNR dangers "knowledge-enabled mass destruction." The BEST paraphrase is that they require:

  1. Vast state facilities and rare uranium.
  2. Mainly knowledge and information, making them accessible to small groups or individuals.
  3. No knowledge at all.
  4. International treaties to build.
Answer & Explanation

B. Accessibility via knowledge is the defining shift from nuclear-era barriers. A describes old WMD; C and D are false.

  4. Biological weapons are safe.

B. It is Joy's proof-of-concept for relinquishment in limited cases. A overstates the difficulty; C and D are false.

  1. The precautionary principle.

B. The coordination problem is relinquishment's classic flaw. The other responses are designed to work despite it.

B. The analogy models cost-effective precaution, not fear. A, C, D miss the analogy.

6. "Differential technological development" (Bostrom) recommends:

B. It steers the mix and order of progress, not just its speed. A, C, D misstate it.

Essay: The Future of Chapter 7's Topics (3 Items)

Each item includes a short model answer to guide checking. Suggested rubric per item: Thesis and stand 20%, Use of chapter concepts (self-replication, existential risk, alignment, precautionary principle) 40%, Evidence and examples 25%, Organization and clarity 15%. Suggested length: 300–500 words each.

1. Bill Joy proposed "relinquishing" certain dangerous technologies. For the coming decades, evaluate whether relinquishment, the precautionary principle, or responsible governance is the wisest overall strategy for humanity's most dangerous technologies — or whether we need a combination. Use concrete examples.

Short Model Answer

A strong answer weighs all three rather than picking a slogan. Relinquishment works for narrowly defined, unusually dangerous cases with international buy-in (biological weapons; arguably lethal autonomous weapons) but fails as a general strategy because of the coordination problem (if one actor refuses, another proceeds) and the forfeited benefits. The precautionary principle is well-suited to severe, irreversible-harm technologies (shifting the burden to proving safety) but can over-restrict beneficial innovation if applied absolutely. Responsible governance — building safety, oversight, and international agreement into development (the nuclear/ozone precedents) — is the mainstream, most broadly workable path, ideally combined with Bostrom's differential development (speed up protective tech, slow the most dangerous). The best essays argue for a combination matched to the risk profile of each technology, and close on the course's theme: the strategy chosen is itself a deliberate human choice, and the gap to close is between power and wisdom.

2. The "alignment problem" holds that the danger of advanced AI is competence in service of the wrong goal, not malice. Argue whether humanity is likely to solve alignment before building systems more capable than ourselves, and what individuals, companies, and governments (including the Philippines) should do now.

Short Model Answer

A strong answer states the problem precisely (goal mis-specification + optimization power; Russell's "danger scales with capability") and takes a qualified stand on the timeline, acknowledging genuine uncertainty and the serious skeptical view (superintelligence may be distant; present harms deserve priority) without dismissing long-term risk. It argues the two are not in competition: invest in safety/alignment research and address today's bias, misinformation, and job impacts. Concrete recommendations by actor: individuals — AI literacy and critical use; companies — safety testing, transparency, human oversight (Chapter 6 guardrails); governments — regulation (EU AI Act model, UNESCO principles), and for the Philippines specifically, the National AI Strategy/NAICRI plus workforce protection given BPO exposure. The best essays conclude that solving alignment "in advance" is prudent regardless of timeline, because the cost of being wrong is asymmetric — and that this, too, is a choice, not a fate.

3. Human enhancement and transhumanism promise to transcend disease, aging, and even our biological limits. Argue whether humanity should pursue radical self-enhancement, addressing the genetic-divide risk, the value (or not) of our "given" nature and mortality, and the Filipino relational concern for community (kapwa).

Short Model Answer

A strong answer engages the real debate rather than caricaturing either side. The bioliberal case: enhancement continues humanity's long project of self-improvement (eyeglasses, vaccines, education) and could relieve immense suffering, so refusing it may be its own harm. The bioconservative case: a genetic divide could split humanity into enhanced and unenhanced classes, catastrophically deepening inequality and threatening equal moral status; the "given" character of life may ground humility, solidarity, and acceptance; and mortality may give life meaning. The kapwa lens sharpens the communal stakes — radical enhancement of some could fracture the bonds of shared humanity. The best essays take a defensible, qualified position (e.g., permit therapeutic and modest enhancements with strict equity safeguards while resisting heritable, society-splitting modifications), tie it to the chapter's "which capacities, for whom, under what conditions, who decides" framework, and note that this is the ultimate "crossing" — technology acting on human nature itself — so it demands the most deliberate collective choice of all.
