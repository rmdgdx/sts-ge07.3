Chapter Test: 20-Item HOTS Multiple Choice

Higher-Order Thinking Skills test — items require analysis, evaluation, inference, and application, not recall. Click "Answer & Explanation" to reveal the key.

1. Society ignored social media's harms until they were entrenched and hard to fix. This is a textbook example of:

  1. Horizon scanning done well.
  2. The Collingridge dilemma — the harms became clear only after the technology was too embedded to easily change.
  3. Dual-use.
  4. Post-quantum cryptography.
Answer & Explanation

B. Late-recognized, hard-to-fix harm is the Collingridge trap. A is the opposite; C and D are different concepts.

2. The Collingridge dilemma implies that responsible governance of a new technology should:

  1. Wait for certain proof of harm before acting.
  2. Engage the technology early, under uncertainty, because that is when it is still shapeable.
  3. Never regulate technology.
  4. Ban all emerging technologies.
Answer & Explanation

B. Acting early under uncertainty is the dilemma's lesson. A guarantees acting too late; C and D are extremes.

3. "The future is already here — it's just not evenly distributed" is used to argue that emerging-tech literacy is:

  1. Impossible, since the future is unknowable.
  2. The skill of seeing frontier technologies clearly before they spread widely, so we can prepare and shape them.
  3. Only for scientists.
  4. Irrelevant to ordinary citizens.
Answer & Explanation

B. The quote frames anticipation as a learnable civic skill. A, C, D dismiss it.

4. Calling generative AI a "general-purpose technology" (like electricity) BEST implies that:

  1. It will affect only the tech industry.
  2. It will reshape nearly every field, so its governance and social effects are broad, not niche.
  3. It is harmless.
  4. It is identical to a calculator.
Answer & Explanation

B. General-purpose = economy-wide impact. A, C, D understate it.

5. The chapter says the key AI question is not "is AI good or bad?" but "what kind of society do we build around it?" This reflects which course-long principle?

  1. Technological determinism.
  2. That technology is a choice-dependent amplifier; outcomes hinge on the social choices around it (co-production).
  3. That AI has no effects.
  4. Scientism.
Answer & Explanation

B. Co-production/choice-dependence is the framing. A is the opposite; C and D are false.

6. A student uses AI to write an essay they were meant to think through themselves. The chapter's lens frames the cost as:

  1. Nothing; efficiency is always good.
  2. Losing something essential (skill, understanding, originality) — AI as a tool must serve human ends, not replace the growth that makes us human (Chs. 4–6).
  3. A violation of copyright only.
  4. Purely a technical error.
Answer & Explanation

B. The power-vs-flourishing distinction applied to learning. A ignores the loss; C and D miss the point.

7. Why is it inaccurate to call a quantum computer simply "a much faster computer"?

  1. Because it is slower.
  2. Because it is powerful for a narrow class of specific problems, not for everything — a different kind of machine, not just a faster one.
  3. Because it uses bits.
  4. Because it does not exist.
Answer & Explanation

B. Quantum advantage is problem-specific. A and C are false; D is inaccurate (early machines exist).

8. The "harvest now, decrypt later" threat is a compelling case for anticipatory governance because it shows that:

  1. Encryption is already broken today.

  3. Quantum computers are harmless.

Answer & Explanation

B. The future threat justifies present action — the Collingridge insight applied. A, C, D are false.

9. Quantum computing "could design life-saving drugs or crack the world's secrets." This BEST illustrates:

Essay: The Future of Chapter 12's Topics (3 Items)

Each item includes a short model answer to guide checking. Suggested rubric per item: Thesis and stand 20%, Use of chapter concepts (Collingridge, dual-use, anticipatory governance) 40%, Evidence and examples 25%, Organization and clarity 15%. Suggested length: 300–500 words each.

1. The Collingridge dilemma holds that technologies are easiest to shape before we know we need to. Choose one emerging technology (generative AI, quantum computing, BCIs, or the new space age) and argue how society should govern it now, given that we cannot yet be certain of its effects. What would anticipatory, human-centered governance concretely require?

Short Model Answer

A strong answer states the Collingridge dilemma precisely and applies it to a chosen technology. For generative AI, for example: because harms (bias, misinformation, job loss, concentration) are becoming visible while the technology is still shapeable, society should act now — through the EU AI Act model, UNESCO principles, human-oversight and transparency requirements, bias auditing, and workforce protection — rather than waiting for certainty. The answer should acknowledge the dilemma's genuine difficulty (acting under uncertainty risks both over- and under-regulation) and argue for adaptive governance that can learn and change, plus the precautionary attention severe/irreversible risks deserve without paralysis. The best essays specify what "human-centered" means (dignity, rights, the common good as the measure; keeping humans in the loop), note the dual-use character of the chosen technology, and connect to the course thesis that the future is shaped by present choices — including for the Philippines (National AI Strategy, workforce, equity).

2. The Philippines faces a choice at the technological frontier: adopter, creator, or left behind. Argue which path the country should pursue over the next two decades across AI, space, and digital security, and what investments, education, and governance it would require — while keeping the common good (kabutihang panlahat) as the measure.

Short Model Answer

A strong answer resists a slogan and defends a realistic, layered strategy. It should acknowledge the constraints (R&D underinvestment, brain drain, digital divide, dependence — Ch. 3) and the assets (young, English-speaking, digitally fluent population; existing institutions like PhilSA, NAICRI, the Innovation/Startup Acts, Pagtanaw 2050). A defensible position: pursue selective creation in areas of national strength and need (locally-relevant AI for agriculture/disaster/health and local languages; earth-observation and disaster satellites via PhilSA; cybersecurity/post-quantum readiness) while being a wise adopter elsewhere — always avoiding being left behind. Requirements: sustained R&D funding toward the ~1% benchmark, education and reskilling (especially given BPO exposure to AI, Ch. 7), data and security governance, and equitable access so benefits reach beyond urban elites. The best essays keep kabutihang panlahat central — insisting frontier technology serve the whole nation and future generations — and frame the choice as a deliberate decision, echoing "development is a decision."

3. As this course's capstone, reflect on whether humanity is likely to prove "not just clever but wise" in governing the converging frontiers of AI, biotechnology, quantum computing, and neurotechnology. Draw on the whole course to argue whether you are ultimately hopeful or fearful about the technological future — and why.

Short Model Answer

A strong answer is genuinely integrative, drawing on multiple chapters, and takes a clear but nuanced stand rather than a slogan. The fearful case: convergence raises pace and stakes; the Collingridge dilemma and manufactured doubt (Ch. 8) make timely governance hard; power tends to concentrate; and history shows society often acts too late (fossil fuels, social media). The hopeful case: humanity has, at times, shown collective wisdom — banning biological weapons and CFCs, the emerging AI-governance movement, the near-universal line against heritable editing (Ch. 10) — proving that "development is a decision" and that we can choose not to use powers we possess. The best essays argue that the outcome is not predetermined (rejecting both techno-utopian and doomer determinism), that it depends on informed, ethical, engaged citizenship — the very capacity this course cultivates — and land on "active hope": justified not by optimism about outcomes but by conviction that the future is a frontier we shape. They should explicitly tie the reflection to the course's throughline that science and technology are human, social activities whose future is up to us.
